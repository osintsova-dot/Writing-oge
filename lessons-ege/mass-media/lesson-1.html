Lesson 1 — Input · B2Mass media: news, networks & the truth

Lesson outcome: the student commands idiomatic B2 vocabulary of the "Mass media" topic (press & broadcasting, social media, the reliability of journalism) and applies the EGE multiple-choice reading and listening strategy without falling for distractors. Lesson flow: the lead-in draws on personal experience of the media → presentation and controlled practice of the vocabulary → transfer of the same vocabulary and topic to a real EGE text (reading, tasks 12–18) → to a real EGE audio (tasks 3–9) → free speech supported by the new vocabulary. Through-line: "how to tell reliable information from noise" runs through every stage. Homework: "Vocabulary" + "Reading" + "Listening" in the EGE trainer for this topic.
1

Lead-in · Discussion

8 min
  • Where do you get your news from — TV, social media, or news sites? Which do you trust most, and why?
  • Have you ever shared a story online and later found out it was false? What happened?
  • Some say social media keeps us informed; others say it just makes us scroll endlessly. What do you think?
Teacher’s note: at B2 the aim of the lead-in is to elicit argumentation (because / on the one hand…), not just to get students talking. Note on the board the media words that come up (news, fake, source, viral) — you’ll return to them in task 2 as words students already "own".

3

Reading · FIPI (tasks 12–18, MC)

22 min

🔎 Real EGE text. Read and answer questions 12–18 (choose 1–4).

My experience with ChatGPT A few weeks ago, I asked ChatGPT to write an article and I have to say, it exceeded my expectations. Not only did ChatGPT write a comprehensive article, but it also included helpful headlines for each section. Since then, I’ve been thinking a lot about Artificial Intelligence (AI) and what it could mean for the future world. I’ve been keeping up with news about AI developments, but ChatGPT really stood out to me. While there are other models out there doing similar things, there must be a reason why ChatGPT made such big headlines. I think it’s because people like me started using AI models for the first time and got very tangible results. However, as useful and unique it can be, I do have some concerns. After receiving the article from ChatGPT, I requested another one using similar keywords. ChatGPT delivered, but the resulting article was 62% similar to the first one. I doubt this would happen if I asked two people to write an article using the same keywords. As humans, we all have unique minds and experiences that shape our thoughts and words. Each person’s creativity is unique because it requires unique brain networks to fire simultaneously. It’s no surprise that ChatGPT lacks originality, since it’s trained on millions of pieces of information from various sources. AI relies on pre-existing information to produce content. In contrast, humans learn in various ways and may draw different conclusions from similar experiences. Another challenge with AI is intolerance. AI algorithms are only as good as the data they’re trained on, which can lead to intolerant results. Microsoft experienced this firsthand in 2016 when their AI chatbot on social networks became racist and misogynistic within 24 hours. Even OpenAI, the creator of ChatGPT, acknowledges the limitations of AI. They warn that it “may occasionally generate incorrect information,” “may occasionally produce harmful instructions or biased content,” and has “limited knowledge of the world and events after 2021.” The last limitation is telling. Is it possible for an AI chatbot to “live in the present?” Today’s reality is so fragmented and dependent on individual perspectives that even humans struggle to identify the truth. How can scientists train an AI model to differentiate truth from falsehood? If this were possible (let alone easy), why haven’t humans mastered this ability yet? I wonder to what extent technological innovation occurs for the sake of innovation itself, rather than to address and solve a specific problem. Are we using AI to solve significant global issues, or are we merely using it to fix inconveniences? Recently, I read news about a robo-dog for people with visual impairment. Equipped with AI, it talks and aids them in navigating cities. Why were living service dogs not good enough? Apparently, they are expensive to train and maintain, so the answer technology offered was – what else? – robots. That would be quite reasonable, but 90% of vision loss is preventable or treatable with spectacles or eye surgery. Millions of people have visual impairment because they don’t have access to such treatments, making it a significant and global problem. Shouldn’t we use our resources to prevent and treat vision loss in the first place? We have built a world so dependent on technology and so obsessed with growth that we are now willing to put a price on the only thing that makes us unique in this world: our brain. While we have not fully comprehended its capabilities, we are trying to make a digital copy of it. Are we sure we know what this means?

12. What does the author think of the article written by ChatGPT?
13. What does the expression “made such big headlines” in paragraph 2 mean?
14. Why are humans creative in unique ways?
15. Why did AI become racist very fast?
16. What does “this ability” in paragraph 7 refer to?
17. Which statement is TRUE?
18. What is the main idea of the last paragraph?
Exam strategy. In MC the answer is ALWAYS supported by the text — look for a paraphrase, not a word-for-word match. The distractor often reuses words from the text (for example, train an AI model) but twists the meaning. First locate the paragraph by a keyword from the question, then check the referent (this ability / this) in that very paragraph.
Real FIPI text (My experience with ChatGPT — AI, the media and the reliability of information; directly on the Mass media topic). Answers: 12→1 · 13→2 · 14→1 · 15→1 · 16→1 · 17→3 · 18→4. Discuss WHERE in the text each answer is confirmed: 13 — "made big headlines" = "widely discussed in the media" (paraphrase); 16 — the referent of "this ability" = "differentiate truth from falsehood". Word-for-word options (question 16: "train AI models") are traps.

Listening · FIPI (tasks 3–9, interview)

15 min

🎧 Real EGE task. Listen to the interview with the journalist twice (you get 50 sec to read the questions); choose 1, 2 or 3.

3. Which of the following is NOT true about Eric James?
4. The programme Eric runs is aimed at …
5. What is the first thing Eric decides on when starting a story?
6. According to Eric, opposing characters create … in a story.
7. Eric dislikes … reactions of his readers to his writing.
8. Eric thinks writers need a lot of …
9. How has becoming a father changed Eric’s work?
Strategy. Use the full 50 seconds on the questions — underline the keywords. In the NOT question (task 3) look for what is NOT in the recording. Listen twice: first for the gist, second to confirm. The correct answer PARAPHRASES the recording (tension → a conflict; solitude → privacy); the distractor often repeats words verbatim.
Real FIPI audio — an interview with Eric James, a journalist at the Washington Post (journalism, story writing; directly on the Mass media topic). Answers: 3→3 · 4→2 · 5→2 · 6→1 · 7→3 · 8→1 · 9→2. Discuss the paraphrases: 6 "tension between characters" = "a conflict"; 8 "solitude" = "privacy". Listening is a MUST in the trainer (homework).

Speaking · Express & argue

12 min

Speak for 2 minutes: "Are social media a reliable source of news, or do they just spread misinformation?" Use at least 5 phrases from section 2 and give arguments for both sides before your own conclusion.
